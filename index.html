Atmospheric Basics structure & drivers

The engine behind every weather system: uneven solar heating creates pressure and temperature differences, which the atmosphere constantly tries to equalize via wind.

Layers

LayerAltitudeNotes
Troposphere0–12 kmNearly all weather occurs here; temp drops with height
Stratosphere12–50 kmContains ozone layer; temp rises with height
Mesosphere50–85 kmColdest layer
Thermosphere85+ kmAurora occur here

Key drivers

  • Solar heating — equator receives more direct energy than poles, driving global circulation (Hadley, Ferrel, Polar cells)
  • Coriolis effect — Earth's rotation deflects moving air right (N. Hemisphere) or left (S. Hemisphere), curving wind and storms
  • Moisture — water vapor stores latent heat; condensation releases it, fueling storms
  • Jet streams — fast upper-level air rivers (250–400 km/h) formed by temperature contrast at the polar and subtropical boundaries; steer surface storm systems

More facts

  • Global wind belts: trade winds (equator to 30°), westerlies (30–60°), polar easterlies (60–90°) — all curved by Coriolis force
  • The ITCZ (Intertropical Convergence Zone) is a low-pressure band near the equator where trade winds meet, fueling near-daily tropical thunderstorms
  • Dew point is the temperature air must cool to for water vapor to condense; a dew point close to air temperature means high humidity and fog risk
  • The adiabatic lapse rate describes how rising air cools: ~9.8°C/km when dry, ~5°C/km when saturated (condensation releases heat, slowing the cooling)
  • Air pressure at sea level averages 1013.25 hPa (29.92 in Hg) and drops roughly by half every 5.5 km of altitude

El Niño & La Niña ENSO cycle

A 2–7 year oscillation in Pacific Ocean surface temperatures that shifts weather patterns worldwide.

  • El Niño — trade winds weaken, warm water that's normally piled up near Asia sloshes back east toward South America, shifting rainfall and suppressing Atlantic hurricane activity via increased wind shear
  • La Niña — trade winds strengthen, cold deep water upwells off South America, typically boosting Atlantic hurricane activity and altering global rainfall/drought patterns
  • ENSO-neutral — conditions near the long-term average, between the two phases
  • Named "El Niño" (the boy/Christ child) by Peruvian fishermen, since warming typically peaked around Christmas
  • Scientists track it via the Oceanic Niño Index (ONI), a 3-month running average of sea surface temperature anomalies in the equatorial Pacific
  • Strong El Niño years (e.g. 1997–98, 2015–16) are linked to global temperature spikes and disrupted monsoons in India and Australia

Pressure Systems & Fronts

Pressure systems

  • Low pressure (cyclone) — air rises, cools, condenses → clouds/precipitation; winds spiral inward, counterclockwise (N. Hemisphere)
  • High pressure (anticyclone) — air sinks, warms, dries → clear skies; winds spiral outward, clockwise (N. Hemisphere)

Fronts (boundaries between air masses)

FrontFormationWeather
Cold frontCold air advances under warm air, forcing it up sharplyBrief, intense storms; sharp temp drop
Warm frontWarm air rides up gently over retreating cold airLong, steady rain; gradual warming
Occluded frontFast cold front catches a warm front, lifting warm air off the groundMixed precipitation
Stationary frontNeither air mass advancesProlonged rain in one area
  • Isobars — lines of equal pressure on a weather map; tightly packed isobars mean a steep pressure gradient and strong winds
  • The geostrophic wind blows parallel to isobars once the pressure-gradient force and Coriolis force balance, well above the friction layer
  • A dry line separates warm moist air from warm dry air (common in the US Southern Plains) and often triggers explosive thunderstorm development, distinct from a temperature-based front

Thunderstorms

Form when three ingredients align: moisture, instability, and lift.

Formation stages

CumulusMatureDissipating
  1. Cumulus stage — warm, moist air rises (updraft); condenses into a growing cumulus cloud
  2. Mature stage — cloud reaches the tropopause (anvil top forms); updraft and downdraft coexist; heaviest rain, lightning, hail, gusts
  3. Dissipating stage — downdraft cuts off the updraft's moisture supply; storm rains itself out

Types

  • Single-cell — short-lived, weak wind shear, isolated
  • Multicell cluster/line — group of cells at different stages; can form squall lines
  • Supercell — a single rotating updraft (mesocyclone) sustained by strong wind shear; produces the most violent tornadoes, giant hail, and long-lived severe weather
  • About 2,000 thunderstorms are occurring on Earth at any given moment — roughly 16 million per year, mostly in tropical regions
  • CAPE (Convective Available Potential Energy) measures how much fuel is available for an updraft; values above ~2,500 J/kg indicate a high potential for severe storms
  • A storm is classed severe (US criteria) if it produces hail ≥ 2.5 cm, wind gusts ≥ 93 km/h, or a tornado

Tornadoes

A violently rotating column of air in contact with both the ground and a cumulonimbus cloud.

Formation (supercell tornadogenesis)

  1. Wind shear (wind changing speed/direction with height) creates horizontal spin in the lower atmosphere
  2. A supercell's updraft tilts this spin vertical, forming a rotating mesocyclone
  3. The rear-flank downdraft wraps cool air around the mesocyclone, tightening and stretching rotation near the surface — vertical stretching conserves angular momentum, spinning the column faster (ice-skater effect)
  4. A funnel condenses and touches down as debris confirms surface circulation

Non-supercell tornadoes (landspouts, waterspouts) form differently — a pre-existing surface spin gets stretched upward by a growing cumulus updraft, without a mesocyclone.

  • The US averages ~1,200 tornadoes a year, far more than any other country — most occur in "Tornado Alley" (Texas to South Dakota) due to collisions between Gulf moisture and dry continental air
  • Doppler radar detects a hook echo — a hook-shaped radar signature marking the rain wrapping around a mesocyclone — as an early tornado warning sign
  • The Tri-State Tornado (1925) holds the record for longest continuous track: 352 km across Missouri, Illinois, and Indiana
  • Waterspouts come in two types: tornadic (a supercell tornado over water) and the more common, weaker fair-weather waterspout forming from a growing cumulus cloud with no thunderstorm

Lightning & Hail

Lightning

Collisions between rising ice crystals and falling graupel (soft hail) inside a storm's mixed-phase region separate electric charge — lighter ice crystals carry positive charge upward, heavier graupel carries negative charge down. This builds a strong charge gradient between the cloud base (negative) and top (positive), and between the cloud and ground (positively charged by induction). When the gradient overcomes air's insulating resistance, a stepped leader of ionized air jumps toward the ground, met by a return stroke that produces the visible flash.

Hail

Forms when strong updrafts carry raindrops far above the freezing level. The frozen droplet cycles up and down through the storm, accreting supercooled water in layers (like an onion) until it grows too heavy for the updraft to support and falls.

  • A lightning bolt reaches roughly 30,000 K — about five times hotter than the sun's surface — which is what causes the explosive expansion of air heard as thunder
  • Count seconds between the flash and the thunder and divide by 3 to estimate distance in kilometers (or by 5 for miles), since light is essentially instant and sound travels ~343 m/s
  • The largest recorded hailstone (Vivian, South Dakota, 2010) was 20 cm in diameter and weighed 0.88 kg

Downbursts & Derechos

Straight-line wind events, often mistaken for tornado damage but caused by air moving down and outward instead of rotating.

  • Downburst — a column of sinking air (driven by evaporative cooling of falling rain, which makes air denser) that slams into the ground and spreads outward in a straight-line burst
  • Microburst — a downburst under 4 km wide, sometimes exceeding 240 km/h; a serious hazard to aircraft on takeoff/landing due to sudden wind-shear reversal
  • Macroburst — a downburst wider than 4 km, causing widespread straight-line damage
  • Derecho — a long-lived, fast-moving band of thunderstorms producing a swath of straight-line wind damage extending over 400 km, with wind gusts of at least 93 km/h along most of its length

Tropical Cyclones (Hurricanes / Typhoons / Cyclones)

Same phenomenon, named by region: hurricane (Atlantic/E. Pacific), typhoon (W. Pacific), cyclone (Indian Ocean/Australia).

Formation requirements

  • Sea surface temperature ≥ 26.5°C to a depth of ~50 m (fuel via evaporation)
  • Pre-existing disturbance/thunderstorm cluster
  • Location > 500 km from equator, so Coriolis force can induce rotation
  • Low vertical wind shear, so the storm's structure isn't torn apart
  • High mid-level humidity

Development stages

Tropical disturbanceTropical depressionTropical stormHurricane/Typhoon

Warm ocean water evaporates, rises, and condenses, releasing latent heat that lowers surface pressure further, drawing in more air — a self-sustaining feedback loop. Coriolis force curves the inflowing air into a spiral. As pressure drops, winds intensify through each named stage above (thresholds: depression <63 km/h, storm 63–118 km/h, hurricane ≥119 km/h).

Structure

  • Eye — calm, clear center of sinking air, typically 30–65 km wide
  • Eyewall — ring of the most intense thunderstorms and winds surrounding the eye
  • Rainbands — spiral bands of showers/storms extending outward, sometimes hundreds of km

Storms weaken over land or cold water because the warm-water energy source is cut off — this is why landfall triggers rapid decay.

  • Rapid intensification is defined as a wind speed increase of ≥ 55 km/h within 24 hours — increasingly common and dangerous since it leaves little time to prepare
  • The eyewall replacement cycle — a new, larger eyewall forms outside the original and chokes it off — briefly weakens a storm before it often re-strengthens with a wider wind field
  • Storm names are recycled on a rotating list every 6 years; a name is permanently retired if the storm was especially deadly or costly (e.g. Katrina, Harvey, Haiyan)
  • Typhoon Tip (1979) is the most intense tropical cyclone on record, with a minimum central pressure of 870 hPa and a wind field over 2,000 km wide
  • A storm losing tropical characteristics (symmetric shape, warm core) as it moves poleward and merges with a frontal system undergoes extratropical transition

Winter Storms & Blizzards

Form when cold, dry polar air clashes with warm, moist air along a front, and a low-pressure system provides lift. The type of precipitation depends on the vertical temperature profile: snow (column below freezing throughout), sleet (melts then refreezes), freezing rain (melts, then meets a thin freezing layer near the surface).

Blizzard criteria (US): sustained winds/gusts ≥ 56 km/h, visibility ≤ 0.4 km, for ≥ 3 hours.

Nor'easters (US East Coast) and lake-effect snow (cold air moving over relatively warm lake water, picking up moisture that freezes as it hits colder land) are regional variants.

  • The polar vortex is a persistent upper-atmosphere low-pressure system over the poles; when it weakens or "wobbles," it can push Arctic air far south
  • Thundersnow occurs when enough instability exists within a snow-producing system to generate lightning — the snow muffles the thunder to a low rumble
  • Snow-to-liquid ratio varies with temperature: roughly 10:1 (10 cm of snow per cm of rain) is typical, but very cold, dry snow can reach 20:1 or higher
  • Wind chill measures how cold exposed skin feels by combining air temperature with wind-driven heat loss, not the air's actual temperature

Floods

  • Flash floods — rapid flooding within hours, from intense rainfall, dam/levee failure, or ice jams; most dangerous due to short warning time
  • River (fluvial) floods — slower buildup from prolonged rain or snowmelt exceeding channel capacity
  • Storm surge — abnormal sea level rise driven onshore by a storm's winds and low pressure; the deadliest hazard in most tropical cyclones
  • A "100-year flood" doesn't mean once a century — it means a flood of that size has a 1% chance of occurring in any given year
  • Atmospheric rivers are narrow corridors of concentrated water vapor transport (like the "Pineapple Express") that can carry more water than the Mississippi River's mouth and cause extreme rainfall on the US West Coast

Drought & Heat Waves

Drought develops from a persistent, abnormal deficit in precipitation, often locked in by stable high-pressure ridges that block storm systems for weeks or months. Heat waves form similarly — a stagnant high-pressure ridge causes sinking air that compresses and warms, suppresses cloud formation, and traps heat at the surface.

  • Heat index combines temperature and humidity to estimate how hot it feels, since high humidity blocks sweat from evaporating and cooling the body
  • Wet-bulb temperature — the lowest temperature achievable by evaporative cooling — is a more dangerous heat metric; above ~35°C wet-bulb, the human body can no longer cool itself even in shade with unlimited water
  • The 1930s "Dust Bowl" in the US Great Plains combined severe drought with poor farming practices, stripping topsoil and causing massive dust storms

Earthquakes

Sudden release of energy stored in the Earth's crust, radiating as seismic waves.

Formation

  1. Tectonic plates move slowly (cm/year), but friction locks fault lines in place
  2. Stress accumulates at the locked section over years to centuries
  3. When stress exceeds the fault's frictional strength, the rock ruptures and the two sides suddenly slip
  4. Stored elastic energy releases as seismic waves radiating from the focus (rupture point underground); the point directly above on the surface is the epicenter

Fault types

  • Normal fault — crust stretching (divergent boundaries)
  • Reverse/thrust fault — crust compressing (convergent boundaries)
  • Strike-slip fault — plates sliding past each other horizontally (e.g. San Andreas)

Seismic waves

WaveTypeNotes
P-wavePrimary, compressionalFastest; arrives first; travels through solid & liquid
S-waveSecondary, shearSlower; ground moves perpendicular to travel; can't pass through liquid outer core
Surface wavesLove & RayleighSlowest, largest amplitude; cause most damage

Moment magnitude (Mw) has replaced the Richter scale for large quakes — it measures total energy released via fault area, slip distance, and rock rigidity. Each whole number increase = ~32× more energy release.

  • The Modified Mercalli Intensity scale measures shaking severity and damage at a specific location (I–XII), unlike magnitude, which is a single fixed value for the whole quake
  • The largest earthquake ever recorded was the 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile, at magnitude 9.5
  • Foreshocks precede the main rupture (recognized only in hindsight), while aftershocks follow as the crust readjusts — sometimes for months or years
  • Liquefaction occurs when intense shaking causes water-saturated, loose soil to temporarily behave like a liquid, making buildings sink or tilt
  • Earthquake early-warning systems detect the faster, less-damaging P-waves and send alerts seconds before the slower, more destructive S-waves and surface waves arrive

Tsunamis

Formation

  1. A sudden vertical displacement of a large water column — most commonly an undersea megathrust earthquake abruptly lifting or dropping the seafloor (also caused by underwater landslides, volcanic collapse, or rarely meteor impacts)
  2. This displacement propagates outward as a series of long-wavelength waves, moving at up to 800 km/h in deep water — but with an amplitude of often under a meter, unnoticeable at sea
  3. Approaching shallow coastal water, the wave slows and its energy compresses into a much shorter wavelength, forcing amplitude (wave height) to rise dramatically — a process called shoaling
  4. Water may first recede noticeably from the shore (trough arrives first) before the crest arrives as a fast-rising surge, not typically a single towering breaking wave
A tsunami arrives as a series of waves over hours — later waves are sometimes larger than the first.
  • The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, triggered by a magnitude 9.1 earthquake off Sumatra, killed roughly 230,000 people across 14 countries — the deadliest tsunami on record
  • The 1883 eruption of Krakatoa generated tsunami waves over 30 m high by violently displacing water as the volcano's caldera collapsed
  • DART buoys (Deep-ocean Assessment and Reporting of Tsunamis) detect pressure changes on the seafloor and relay data via satellite to give coastal areas minutes to hours of warning

Volcanoes

Formation settings

  • Convergent boundaries — one plate subducts beneath another, melting into magma that rises (e.g. Pacific Ring of Fire)
  • Divergent boundaries — plates pull apart, magma rises to fill the gap (e.g. mid-ocean ridges, Iceland)
  • Hotspots — a stationary plume of magma rises through the crust regardless of plate boundaries; the plate moves over it, creating volcanic chains (e.g. Hawaii)

Eruption style depends on magma

  • Effusive — low-silica, low-viscosity, low-gas magma flows out relatively gently (shield volcanoes, e.g. Kilauea)
  • Explosive — high-silica, viscous magma traps gas until pressure ruptures it violently, ejecting ash and pyroclastic material (stratovolcanoes, e.g. Mt. St. Helens)

Secondary hazards: pyroclastic flows (fast-moving superheated gas/ash avalanches), lahars (volcanic mudflows from melted ice/ash mixing with water), and ashfall disrupting aviation and agriculture.

  • About 75% of the world's active volcanoes lie along the Pacific "Ring of Fire," tracing the subduction zones circling the Pacific Plate
  • A supervolcano is capable of a VEI-8 eruption; Yellowstone's caldera formed from such an eruption roughly 640,000 years ago and could trigger a global "volcanic winter" if it erupted again
  • Volcanic lightning can form within an ash plume as ash particles and ice collide and generate static charge, similar to a thunderstorm
  • The 79 AD eruption of Mt. Vesuvius buried Pompeii under several meters of ash and pumice, preserving the city almost intact for centuries
